Fire forgets which way is up.

Drag the gravity control. On Earth, hot gas rises and pulls the flame into a tall yellow teardrop. In orbit nothing rises, oxygen creeps in from every side, and the flame curls into a dim blue sphere. Then switch the fans off and watch.

The log · a training story

Forty-five minutes at Shackleton Station

A made-up fire at a base near the Moon's south pole. On one side, what the crew said. On the other, the real NASA result that explains it.

FICTIONThis is an imagined drill set in 2041. Names and small numbers are ours, but every behavior in it was seen in a real NASA experiment or a real spaceflight fire.

  1. T−00:00
    Station system

    Cabin atmosphere nominal. O₂ 30% at 10.2 psi.

    Challenge brief

    Lunar habitats and pressurized rovers are expected to run on 30% oxygen or more at ≤ 10.2 psi. That's over 40% more oxygen share than Earth air, so materials ignite more easily and burn harder.

  2. T+00:00
    Station system

    Wire bundle behind panel C-4 overheating. No alarm.

    SAME

    Much of the smoke from overheated spacecraft materials was under 500 nanometres. Neither the shuttle's ionization detector nor the ISS's photoelectric one caught every type of smoke reliably.

  3. T+01:30
    Engineer Nour

    “I can smell something hot, and the detector is quiet.”

    SAME

    In low gravity smoke doesn't climb to the ceiling, so ISS detectors sit inside the ventilation ducts where air is pulled through. The crew's own senses are still part of detection.

  4. T+02:10
    Engineer Nour

    “There's a flame on the fabric sleeve. Small, rounded, almost blue. I can barely see it.”

    ACME · FLEX

    With little buoyancy, less soot forms, and glowing soot is what makes Earth flames yellow. Low-gravity flames are dimmer and bluer, which makes them easier to miss.

  5. T+02:40
    Commander Youssef

    “That sleeve passed its flammability test on Earth. Why is it spreading?”

    BASS-II · Saffire

    In BASS-II flames survived at 17% O₂ in microgravity while the same material needed 18% on Earth, and sometimes spread faster. Saffire showed Earth test results don't always carry over to a spacecraft.

  6. T+03:00
    Commander Youssef

    “Kill the cabin fans. Now.”

    BASS

    Across 115 burns on the ISS, switching the airflow off put flames out quickly. Faster air made flames spread faster, and near 1 cm/s they starved.

  7. T+03:25
    Nour / Commander Youssef

    “Flame's gone.” “It isn't out until the sensors say so.”

    FLEX

    FLEX discovered “cool flames”: after the visible flame died, fuel kept burning unseen at about 400–1,000 °F, making carbon monoxide and formaldehyde.

  8. T+04:00
    Station system

    CO rising at panel C-4. Crew on masks. Extinguisher into the panel.

    Mir 1997

    In February 1997 an oxygen generator caught fire on Mir with six people aboard. It burned for about 14 minutes and the crew used three extinguishers.

  9. T+45:00
    Station system

    Air scrubbed. Smoke cleared. Incident closed.

    Mir 1997

    On Mir, thick smoke hung in the station for about 45 minutes. What that crew learned went into how the ISS was designed and run.

1

Stop the air

In microgravity the fans are the flame's lungs. Turning them off comes first.

2

Don't trust the dark

No visible flame doesn't mean no fire. Measure CO and gases before calling it out.

3

Test where it will fly

Earth tests can understate risk. Materials need testing at realistic gravity, oxygen and size.

Back on Earth

What orbit teaches, homes keep

Suppression

Water-mist extinguishers

Fine mist puts out fires with far less water. Knowing how flames die without gravity helps tune it.

Detection

Sharper smoke detectors

SAME showed smoke particles are tiny and some detectors miss them. That matters for early warning in buildings too.

Protection

Firefighter gear

Flame-resistant fabrics like Nomex were burned in space, which shows how protective materials hold up.

Energy

Cleaner engines

The cool flames found in FLEX could help design engines that burn fuel more cleanly.
